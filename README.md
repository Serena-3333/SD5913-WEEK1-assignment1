# SD5913-WEEK1-assignment1# Why Are We Here?

To answer the question, I have to talk about my journey to POLYU. I came to this major sideways. My undergraduate degree is in urban and rural planning，which is a field that borrows from geography, sociology, economics, and design without being any of them. When I decided I wanted to work in games, I avoided environment art because it felt too close to the architecture I was trying to leave behind. Instead, I started learning development tools, animation, and code.

I could not write code. So I did what anyone in 2026 does: I described what I wanted, and the machine wrote it for me. This is vibecoding, and for a while it felt like magic. I asked for a generative animation, and I got one. I asked for a character controller, and I got one.

Then I hit the place where describing runs out. For my first game, I needed a trigger animation: when the player's character reached a certain spot, an item message should pop up — quickly several in a row — and then all of them should be destroyed five seconds later. I described exactly this to the AI. The code it wrote did the first half perfectly: the messages appeared, stacked up fast, filled the screen. Then nothing destroyed them. They spilled past the edges, over every UI element, and finally Unity crashed and shut down. I had asked for a *release*, and the machine had only heard the *accumulate*. The flaw was not in my sentence — it was visible, structurally, in a single lifetime method I could not read. Because I could not read it, I tried the same fix again and again, differently phrased, and it never landed. All I could do was sense the gap, and keep rolling the dice.

That gap is why I am here. The assignment asks why a designer should learn to do a thing a machine will increasingly do on request. My answer is specific: because vibe coding has a ceiling, and the ceiling is reading. The machine can write the code, But someone has to look at what it produced and know whether it is any good; not inside the terms the machine offered ("it implements a pop-up"), but inside the ones that are actually doing the work on the screen. That judgment is not something you can ask the machine for, because the machine will always tell you its output is fine.

Maybe what survives is not the writing. Matt Welsh wrote in *Communications of the ACM* that "programming will be obsolete," replaced by systems that are trained rather than written. I am not going to argue with him; much of the syntax I could learn may well be dead within a career. But there is an old rhythm worth remembering. When photography arrived, it was supposed to end painting — what survived was not the craft of reproducing reality, but everything a camera could not take from the hand: subjective sensation, the record of a human decision. Painting did not survive the camera as itself. It survived as what only it could do. The same held when the first computer-art works appeared in 1965 and their makers were accused of building machines to replace artists; the accusation faded, and what remained was a medium people learned to read closely and say something new through.

Reading is the skill the machine cannot hand over. Bret Victor argues that programming "is a way of thinking, not a rote skill," and that you can only work well with a medium you can understand. A film director does not operate the camera, but must understand lenses, lighting, and editing well enough to look at a take and say: this is flat, this is wrong, this is almost there — not because she read the manual, but because she has spent years learning to *read* images until the machine's defaults stopped hiding. My dusk scene went generic not because the AI failed, but because I could not see the choices it had made for me — and, more importantly, the choices it never considered. Reading code is the same act aimed at the same gap.

Ada Lovelace is the ancestor of this claim. Writing in 1843 about Babbage's Analytical Engine, she looked past the machine's stated purpose — calculation — and saw something its own inventor had not: a machine for manipulating symbols, "the entire material of analysis." She had no code to run; there was almost none to write. She *read* the machine, and out of that reading saw a future that barely existed yet. That is the skill I came here to build — not to write faster than a machine, but to read well enough to know when to trust it, when to reject it, and when to push it further.
